### ★ link_strength_histogram

![link_strength_histogram](link_strength_histogram.png)

All 397 feeding links sorted by the share of the predator's diet each one carries, on a log scale. Most links fall between about 0.01 and 0.3, and the tail runs all the way down to 10⁻⁶, so the majority of them carry very little of anything's diet. This is worth including because it explains why strong and weak links behave so differently when they are cut. A few links carry an entire diet on their own while most carry almost nothing, and that lopsidedness is what the link removal results end up measuring.

### ★ total_degree_histogram

![total_degree_histogram](total_degree_histogram.png)

Total degree (prey plus predators) for each of the 43 groups, with the dashed line showing what a random web of the same size and number of links would give. The real spread is much wider than random (variance over mean of 3.80 against 0.78), so the groups are far less uniform than chance would produce. What matters for the removal results, though, is that the most connected group (Benthic cephalopods, with 39 links) still only has about twice the average of 18.5, so there is no hub sitting far above everything else. That is why removing the most connected groups first (R50 = 0.44) ends up barely different from removing them at random (R50 = 0.48).
