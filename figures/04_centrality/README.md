### ★ tl_vs_n_predators

![tl_vs_n_predators](tl_vs_n_predators.png)

The same plot for number of predators (out-degree), again over the 40 consumers, with ρ = −0.65 (p = 4.7 × 10⁻⁶). Here the trend runs the other way, as groups low in the web like Shrimps (27 predators), Benthic cephalopods (24), and Macrozooplankton (24) are eaten by a lot of things, while the groups at the top have few predators or none at all. It is worth putting next to the previous figure, though, because on its own total degree against trophic level gives ρ = −0.07 (p = 0.67), which looks like there is no relationship at all. What is actually happening is that these two strong relationships are cancelling each other out, so the answer depends entirely on which kind of connectedness you measure.

### ★ tl_vs_n_prey

![tl_vs_n_prey](tl_vs_n_prey.png)

Number of prey (in-degree) against trophic level for the 40 consumer groups, with a Spearman ρ of +0.75 (p = 2.9 × 10⁻⁸). The relationship is clearly positive, as the groups near the top (Sharks with 25 prey, Demersal piscivores with 23, and Adult hake with 21) eat far more kinds of prey than the ones near the bottom. This is the figure that tests our hypothesis directly, since we predicted upper-level consumers would show greater diet diversity than primary producers and low-level consumers. The three basal groups are left out, as they have no prey by definition and would only sit tied at the bottom of both rankings and push ρ up without adding anything.
